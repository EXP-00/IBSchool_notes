---

## 1. 📌 BOOK PASSPORT (Context & Purpose)

- **Historical Context:** Written during the rise of the American Religious Right and Reaganism in the 1980s. Atwood drew on historical examples (Puritan settlements, Nazi Germany, the Iranian Revolution) to ground the novel in *real* historical events—she famously called it "speculative fiction," not science fiction, because *"all the things in it have been done before."*
- **Author’s Mission:** To explore what happens when patriarchal theocracy replaces democracy, and to critique the stripping of women's rights, bodily autonomy, and literacy.
- **Key Inspirations:** The Salem witch trials, the treatment of women under the Taliban, and 17th-century Puritanism.

---

## 2. 🏗️ STRUCTURAL MAP (The "How")

- **Narrative Voice:** First-person, present-tense, **unreliable** narration (Offred filters everything through her memory and trauma).  
- **Structure:** **Non-linear.** Juxtaposes:
  - **Present Tense (Gilead):** Sterile, claustrophobic, ritualistic.
  - **Past Tense (Flashbacks):** Memories of her husband Luke, her daughter, her job, and the pre-Gilead world.
- **Key Turning Points:** 
  1. Offred's affair with Nick (the first act of real rebellion).
  2. The visit to Jezebel's (exposes the hypocrisy of the regime).
  3. The arrival of the "Mayday" black van (ambiguous ending).
- **Opening vs. Ending Contrast:** 
  - **Opening:** *"We slept in the old gymnasium..."* — passive, collective, dehumanized.
  - **Ending:** *"I would like to believe this is a story I'm telling..."* / The "Historical Notes" epilogue. The ending deliberately destabilizes reality, asking if she escaped or was recaptured/killed.

## 3. 👤 CHARACTER PROFILES (Function over Traits)

| Character | Role / Archetype | Key Traits | What do they Represent? | Development (Arc) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Offred** | Protagonist / Unreliable Narrator | Nostalgic, passive, observant, resilient | The ordinary individual crushed by the State; Memory as a tool of resistance; The struggle for agency. | Moves from survival-mode passivity to active rebellion (sleeping with Nick, hoping for Mayday). |
| **The Commander** | Antagonist / Patriarch | Boring, lonely, hypocritical, pathetic | The banality of evil; Men who create oppressive systems for "order" but secretly crave intimacy and transgression. | Reveals his vulnerability (playing Scrabble, asking her to read) - humanizes the oppressor, making him more terrifying. |
| **Serena Joy** | Commander's Wife / Antagonist | Bitter, cold, former televangelist | Internalized misogyny; Women who uphold patriarchy for status; The "wife" archetype stripped of power. | Her arc declines from power to desperation (she gives Offred her cigarette, ultimately sets up the pregnancy plot). |
| **Moira** | Foil to Offred | Rebellious, pragmatic, overtly sexual, unbroken | Uncompromised female resistance; Queer identity erased by Gilead. | Contrasts Offred—she actively escapes (twice), showing that resistance is possible, but ends up in Jezebel's, showing the limits of rebellion. |
| **Nick** | Love interest / Ambiguous figure | Mysterious, quiet, brooding | The ambiguous male ally; The "resistance" from within the regime. | Moves from a passive Guardian to an active participant in her possible escape. |

## 4. 🧠 THEMES & GLOBAL ISSUES (The "What")

> **Crucial Rule:** For every theme, immediately note the **authorial choice** Atwood uses to present it.

| Theme / Global Issue | Authorial Choice Used | Effect on the Reader |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Power & Oppression** | *Totalitarian language* (e.g., "Blessed be the fruit," "Praise be"). | Strips individuality; forces uniform responses; shows how regime controls thought through speech. |
| **Identity & Selfhood** | *First-person, fragmented narrative* (switching between past and present). | Mirrors how trauma fractures identity; Offred cannot form a stable sense of self because her past is erased. |
| **Gender & Patriarchy** | *Segregation of roles* (Handmaids, Wives, Marthas, Aunts). | Exposes the absurdity of gender essentialism; criticizes how patriarchal systems categorize women by biological function (fertility). |
| **Memory & Storytelling** | *Metafictional moments* ("I tell this story to survive..."). | Stories become the ultimate act of resistance against erasure. |
| **Religion as Weapon** | *Intertextuality* (twisting Biblical passages, e.g., Rachel and Bilhah). | Critiques how sacred texts are selectively interpreted to justify political agendas. |

## 5. 🧰 THE "TOOLBOX" (Authorial Choices by Technique)

| Technique | Examples from the Book | Effect / Purpose |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Restricted Sensory Imagery** | Offred describes smells (scent of the Commander's study, the garden) and limited visual fields (she only sees through the veil). | Her senses are the only rebellion left to her; emphasizes the claustrophobia and sensory deprivation of Gilead. |
| **Semantic Field of Prison** | "We were a colony of ants," "the Wall," "the Salvaging." | Dehumanizes the Handmaids; frames Gilead as a total institution (like a prison or concentration camp). |
| **Parataxis (Short, staccato sentences)** | *"I lie. I wait. I sleep. I eat."* | Mimics the monotony and mechanical existence of the Handmaids; devoid of emotional connection. |
| **Symbolism of Mirrors/Windows** | Offred looks at herself but the mirrors are removed; she looks out of windows constantly. | Mirrors = loss of self-knowledge; Windows = longing for freedom and the outside world. |
| **Epilogue (Historical Notes)** | Set in 2195, a university symposium discussing Gilead as a historical artifact. | De-stabilizes the ending; suggests academics trivialize female suffering; mocks how history is recorded by the powerful. |

> *Only record single, potent phrases. Keep analysis to 1 sentence.*

| Quote (with Page) | Theme | Technique | 1-Sentence Analysis |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| *"We slept in the old gymnasium, on cots. They told us it was for our safety."* [p. 3] | Oppression / Deception | Euphemism & Biblical allusion | The phrase "for our safety" is a chilling euphemism; Atwood exposes how totalitarian regimes cloak imprisonment as protection. |
| *"I wait, I compose myself. My self is a thing I must now compose."* [p. 54] | Identity | Active verb "compose" | Offred sees her identity as a constructed, fragile artwork, not an innate quality—highlighting Gilead's erasure of selfhood. |
| *"Nolite te bastardes carborundorum."* [p. 89] | Resistance / Solidarity | Latin motto (syntax defacement) | The act of writing (even graffiti) is defiance; this hidden message gives Offred a sense of connection to the previous Handmaid and shared rebellion. |
| *"How could I have believed, ever, that I was a person?"* [p. 65] | Dehumanization | Rhetorical question | Highlights the psychological violence of Gilead—Offred internalizes her objectification to the point of questioning her own humanity. |
| *"I would like to believe this is a story I'm telling."* [p. 312] | Memory / Metafiction | Meta-narrative (self-reflexive) | Atwood forces the reader to confront the nature of storytelling itself—is Offred writing for survival, or is this all a delusion? |

## 7. 🔗 CONNECTIONS (Crucial for Paper 2)

> *Compare this book to your other Paper 2 texts. Fill this in based on your syllabus.*

- **Similarities with [Text B, e.g., *1984*]:** 
  - Both use totalitarian regimes that control language to control thought (Newspeak vs. Gilead's liturgical language).
  - Both feature protagonists who find rebellion through forbidden relationships.
  
- **Differences with [Text B, e.g., *1984*]:**
  - *1984* is male-centric and focuses on political power; *The Handmaid's Tale* is explicitly feminist and focuses on gendered oppression and reproduction, not just surveillance.
  
- **Similarities with [Text C, e.g., *A Streetcar Named Desire*]:**
  - Both explore the vulnerability of women in male-dominated spaces; both use unreliable narration/memory to show psychological breakdown.
