#### Confirmation Bias

Seeking or interpreting information in a way that supports what you already believe.

**TOK example:** A student only reads articles that agree with their political opinion.

#### Selection Bias

Choosing evidence or data in a way that makes the conclusion unrepresentative.

**TOK example:** A survey about school lunches only asks students who like the cafeteria food.

#### Availability Bias

Giving more importance to information that is easy to remember or recall.

**TOK example:** After hearing about a plane crash, someone thinks flying is extremely dangerous.

#### Anchoring Bias

Relying too heavily on the first piece of information encountered.

**TOK example:** Seeing "$100" as the original price makes a "$70" price seem cheap.

#### Hindsight Bias

Believing an event was more predictable after it has happened.

**TOK example:** After a scientific discovery, claiming that "it was obvious" it would happen.

#### Self-Serving Bias

Attributing successes to yourself but failures to external factors.

**TOK example:** "I got a good grade because I'm smart, but I failed because the teacher was unfair."

#### Authority Bias

Trusting information because it comes from an authority figure.

**TOK example:** Accepting a scientific claim simply because a famous scientist said it.

#### Cultural Bias

Judging knowledge using assumptions from your own culture.

**TOK example:** Assuming one culture's definition of "normal" behaviour applies universally.

#### Personal Bias

Individual experiences, beliefs, or preferences influence judgment.

**TOK example:** Disliking a historical interpretation because it conflicts with your personal experiences.

#### Group Bias

Being influenced by the beliefs and opinions of a group you belong to.

**TOK example:** Agreeing with classmates because everyone else supports the same interpretation.

#### Survivorship Bias

Focusing on successful cases while ignoring failures.

**TOK example:** Studying successful entrepreneurs and concluding that their habits guarantee success.

#### Observer Bias

A researcher's expectations influence how they observe or interpret results.

**TOK example:** A researcher unconsciously records observations that support their hypothesis.

#### Sampling Bias

A sample does not adequately represent the wider population.

**TOK example:** Using only university students to make claims about all adults.

#### Reporting Bias

Some results are more likely to be reported or emphasized than others.

**TOK example:** A study emphasizes statistically significant results while ignoring insignificant results.

#### Framing Bias

The way information is presented changes how people perceive it.

**TOK example:** "90% survival rate" can feel different from "10% mortality rate."

#### Recency Bias

Giving disproportionate importance to the most recent information.

**TOK example:** Assuming a sports team is much better because of its latest win.

#### Status Quo Bias

Preferring existing beliefs or practices because they are familiar.

**TOK example:** Rejecting a new scientific theory because the old theory has been accepted for decades.

#### Attribution Bias

Explaining behaviour using overly simplistic or incorrect causes.

**TOK example:** Blaming someone's poor grade on laziness without considering their circumstances.
